01. Executive Summary 
This project presents the complete design, analysis, and protection strategy of a corrosion-resistant 
marine propulsion shaft intended for long-term operation in a high-salinity seawater environment. 
The shaft operates within the immersion and splash zones, where chloride exposure, cyclic torsional 
loading, oxygen differentials, and biofouling significantly accelerate localized corrosion and fatigue 
deterioration. 
A systematic design methodology was followed, beginning with a detailed assessment of the marine 
environment, the identification of governing corrosion mechanisms, and quantification of mechanical 
design constraints. Material selection was carried out using a structured Translate–Screen–Rank–
Document approach within ANSYS Granta EduPack, filtering over 1900 candidate materials and 
ultimately selecting Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 due to its high yield strength (~460–510 MPa), 
excellent pitting resistance (PREN > 30), favourable fatigue performance, and superior reliability 
under chloride exposure. 
Mechanical design involved calculating the required outer and inner shaft diameters based on 
combined torsional, bending, and axial loads, supported by class-rule checks using IACS UR M68 and 
DNV-CG-0038. Fatigue and vibration analyses confirmed that the selected hollow shaft geometry (OD 
= 125 mm, ID = 60 mm) provides adequate safety margins against torsional fatigue, lateral whirling, 
and dynamic misalignment. 
To safeguard performance in the aggressive marine environment, a hybrid corrosion-protection 
strategy was developed. This includes duplex stainless steel as the base material, a multilayer coating 
system (epoxy + polyurethane + antifouling), and supplementary cathodic protection aligned with 
DNV and ABS guidelines. Special attention is given to critical regions such as splines, tapers, and 
bearing journals, which require electropolishing or dry-film lubrication rather than paint. 
Manufacturing and QA plans were prepared to ensure dimensional accuracy, structural integrity, and 
operational safety. These include CNC machining, controlled heat treatment, surface preparation, and 
rigorous non-destructive examination (UT, MPI/DPI) followed by runout verification. All processes 
adhere to international standards (e.g., DNV, ABS, ISO 31000). 
Overall, the report integrates engineering design, corrosion science, materials engineering, and marine-
class requirements to deliver a propulsion shaft that is structurally sound, corrosion-resistant, 
manufacturable, and compliant with marine safety regulations.

03. Application Description 
The marine propulsion shaft operates in one of the most aggressive natural environments encountered 
in engineering applications. Its performance and long-term durability are directly influenced by the 
chemical, physical, and biological characteristics of seawater, as well as the electrochemical processes 
that arise at the metal–seawater interface. Understanding these environmental factors is essential for 
selecting appropriate materials, defining geometric features, predicting degradation modes, and 
designing an effective corrosion protection strategy. 
This section provides a detailed description of the operating environment of the shaft and the 
associated corrosion challenges. Section 3.1 presents an environmental analysis of the marine 
conditions relevant to the propulsion system, including salinity, temperature, dissolved gases, flow 
characteristics, biofouling activity, and other parameters that influence corrosion behavior. Section 3.2 
builds on this foundation by examining the corrosion mechanisms most likely to affect marine 
propulsion shafts, such as pitting, galvanic corrosion, crevice corrosion, microbiologically influenced 
corrosion, and corrosion fatigue. 
Together, these analyses establish the environmental context required for subsequent sections on 
material selection, mechanical design, and corrosion protection strategy. They also provide the 
scientific basis for evaluating the suitability of candidate materials such as duplex stainless steels and 
for justifying the adoption of coatings, cathodic protection, or hybrid protection systems in the final 
shaft design. 
 
03. 1 Environmental Analysis of the Marine Operating Environment  
Seawater covers approximately 70% of the Earth’s surface and represents one of the most chemically 
and biologically complex natural environments. It contains nearly every element in the periodic table, 
along with a vast diversity of living organisms, earning it the description of a “living” medium. Due to 
its aggressive nature, seawater is widely regarded as one of the most corrosive environments 
encountered in engineering applications. This chapter explores the critical environmental parameters of 
seawater that influence material degradation, especially corrosion. These factors are essential for 
understanding and predicting material performance in marine systems such as ships, offshore 
platforms, pipelines, and submerged components. The subsections below examine key characteristics 
including salinity, dissolved gases, pH, biological activity, temperature, depth, flow velocity, and 
electrical resistivity, all of which collectively define the corrosive nature of seawater. 
 
03.1.1 Seawater Constituents 
Seawater is widely recognized as one of the most chemically aggressive environments for metallic 
materials, primarily due to its complex composition. Although sodium chloride (NaCl) is often cited as 
the main source of corrosivity, the actual corrosion behavior is influenced more significantly by other 
constituents particularly dissolved oxygen (DO) and pollutants such as sulfides. These components 
critically affect the electrochemical conditions at the metal–seawater interface. 
The total salt content of seawater is expressed as salinity, which refers to the mass of total dissolved 
solids (TDS) in one kilogram of seawater, after oxidation of organic matter and replacement of 
bromine and iodine by chlorine. A related and analytically more accessible parameter is chlorinity, 
which includes the chloride ion concentration as well as the equivalent amount of bromide and iodide. 
The relationship between the two is defined by the empirical equation:

Both salinity and chlorinity are commonly reported in grams per kilogram (g/kg) or parts per thousand 
(ppt or %). To convert these two parts per million (ppm), the values are multiplied by 1,000. Salinity is 
also considered equivalent to TDS and may be expressed in mg/L (ppm). 
Seawater properties vary significantly depending on geographical location, season, and water depth. 
Influencing factors include: 
• River discharge 
• Evaporation and rainfall 
• Melting of polar ice 
• Wind and wave activity 
For instance, littoral (coastal) waters often exhibit lower salinity due to freshwater influx and may also 
contain high levels of sediment and organic/inorganic pollutants, leading to brackish conditions. 
The average salinity of open ocean water is typically 32–36 ppt, and the corresponding chlorinity is 
approximately 18–20 ppt. By contrast, extreme environments such as the Dead Sea exhibit salinities 
more than ten times higher, reaching 322,000 ppm, as shown in Table 1. [1]

From a corrosion standpoint, chloride ions (Cl⁻) are especially aggressive, as they interfere with the 
formation and stability of passive oxide films on metals, particularly stainless steels and aluminum 
alloys. Sulfate ions (SO₄²⁻), though less directly corrosive, support the activity of sulfate-reducing 
bacteria (SRB) under anaerobic conditions, potentially leading to severe microbial corrosion. 
In addition to these ions, seawater contains organic matter that supports biological activity. Elements 
such as carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, and phosphorus are involved in forming polysaccharides,

lipids, and proteins. While carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen are abundant, nitrogen and phosphorus 
compounds (e.g., nitrates and phosphates) often act as limiting nutrients. 
In coastal areas, nutrient loading from river discharge, sewage, and fertilizer runoff can significantly

•

•

Design Relevance of Seawater Constituents 
The chemical composition of seawater, particularly the concentration of aggressive ions plays a central 
role in shaping the corrosion behavior of materials used in marine propulsion shafts. In our project, the 
shaft operates under continuous exposure to seawater immersion and splashing at an average 
temperature of 25 °C, where both galvanic and pitting corrosion are critical threats. Understanding 
seawater constituents helps us make informed decisions regarding material selection, protection 
strategies, and service life prediction. 
Key Impacts on Shaft Design:  
1. High Chloride Content (~19,000 ppm Cl⁻): 
• 
Chloride ions are the primary drivers of pitting corrosion, especially in passive alloys like 
SS316L or Duplex 2205. [1] 
• 
They degrade protective oxide films, making them highly relevant in areas such as the splash 
zone and shaft surface crevices. 
• 
This supports the selection of duplex or NiCrMo alloys with high PREN (Pitting Resistance 
Equivalent Number) values for critical locations. 
2. Bicarbonate and Calcium Ions (HCO₃⁻, Ca²⁺): 
• 
These ions contribute to calcium carbonate (CaCO3) scale formation, particularly in heated or 
moving components. [1] 
• 
While thin scales can reduce general corrosion by limiting DO diffusion, they also create risks 
for under-deposit corrosion and reduce heat transfer at bearing interfaces. 
3. Sulfate Ions and Nutrient Loading (SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻, NO₃⁻): 
• 
Sulfates support the growth of sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB), a common cause of

03.1.2 Effect of Dissolved Gases 
Dissolved gases, particularly dissolved oxygen (DO), are among the most critical factors influencing 
corrosion behavior in seawater. In the context of our marine propulsion shaft which operates in aerated 
seawater at 25 °C under splashing and immersion conditions the role of oxygen is directly linked to 
corrosion initiation, propagation, and even protection system effectiveness. 
 
Key Impacts on Shaft Design: 
1. Dissolved Oxygen Accelerates Corrosion: 
• 
DO acts as the primary cathodic reactant in electrochemical corrosion cells. It facilitates the 
reduction reaction that balances metal dissolution at the anodic sites. 
• 
This makes the splash zone and areas near the waterline, where oxygen access is highest, 
particularly susceptible to general corrosion, pitting, and crevice corrosion all critical for shaft 
surfaces and couplings. 
2. DO Concentration Varies with Temperature and Salinity: 
• 
DO levels decrease with increasing temperature and salinity, both relevant in our case (25 °C 
and ~35 ppt salinity). Despite lower oxygen solubility, turbulent flow around the rotating shaft 
maintains high DO transport, sustaining corrosion activity. 
• 
Figures from the source show this relationship, reinforcing the need to simulate realistic DO 
levels in lab testing. 
3. Effect of Flow and Splashing (High Velocity): 
• 
The high-speed rotation of the shaft (1200 RPM) creates significant water turbulence, 
enhancing oxygen diffusion to metal surfaces. 
• 
While this may benefit passivation in duplex or NiCrMo alloys, it also amplifies localized 
corrosion risk if passive films break down, especially in areas prone to erosion-corrosion or 
debris entrapment.[2] 
4. Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) and Stagnant Zones: 
• 
In partially stagnant areas like keyways, seal interfaces, or coupling recesses, biological 
activity can deplete oxygen locally, setting up differential aeration cells. 
• 
The oxygen-rich outer surface becomes cathodic, while the shielded zone becomes anodic, 
promoting pitting and crevice corrosion especially relevant if stainless steels like SS316L are 
used.

03.1.3 Seawater pH 
Natural seawater is slightly alkaline, with a typical pH range of 8.0 to 8.3, depending on temperature, 
salinity, biological activity, and atmospheric interactions. The carbonate system in seawater involving 
carbon dioxide (CO2), carbonic acid (H2CO3), bicarbonate (HCO3⁻), and carbonate (CO3²⁻) is the 
principal buffer that stabilizes the pH of seawater. 
This system is governed by the following reactions: 
1. CO2 (g) ⇌ CO₂ (aq) 
2. CO2 (aq) + H₂O ⇌ H2CO3 
3. H2CO3 ⇌ H⁺ + HCO3⁻ 
4. HCO3⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO3²⁻ 
The combination of these equilibria provides seawater with its significant buffering capacity. This 
buffering resists abrupt pH shifts when acids or bases are introduced, thus maintaining relatively stable 
pH values in oceanic waters. [3] 
In open-ocean conditions, this system is very effective. However, in coastal regions, especially semi-
enclosed basins, the buffering capacity can be overwhelmed by the influx of: 
• 
Organic material 
• 
Agricultural runoff 
• 
Industrial discharge 
• 
Biological productivity (eutrophication) 
In such areas, local reductions in pH (acidification) are possible. This has significant consequences for: 
• 
Marine organisms, especially calcifies 
• 
Electrochemical processes on metal surfaces 
• 
Corrosion protection systems 
In addition to pH, dissolved oxygen (DO) plays a key role in the corrosion process. DO levels in 
seawater vary with temperature and salinity and are closely linked to pH stability and corrosion 
severity. Two key relationships are illustrated in the following figures:

Bolted assemblies

Seals

Shaft couplings 
• 
Shaft-hull interfaces

In the context of our marine propulsion shaft, which is exposed to splashing and immersion in

like Duplex 2205 and NiCrMo grades. 
However, specific shaft features including keyways, flanges, sealing zones, and stagnant fluid traps are 
prone to localized acidification. This makes them vulnerable to crevice corrosion, especially if 
materials like SS316L are used without enhanced protection. The issue is further exacerbated by 
biofouling or microbial activity, both of which can reduce local pH and oxygen levels.[3]

03.1.4 Calcareous Deposits 
When metals are subjected to cathodic protection in seawater, either through impressed current 
systems or sacrificial anodes, calcareous deposits typically form on their surfaces. These deposits 
consist mainly of: 
• 
Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) 
• 
Magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)2) 
They are produced as a consequence of the electrochemical reactions that occur at the protected 
(cathodic) metal surface. Specifically, the cathodic reduction of oxygen or water leads to an increase in 
local hydroxyl ion (OH⁻) concentration, raising the pH near the metal interface. This localized 
alkalinity promotes the precipitation of calcium and magnesium salts, naturally present in seawater. 
The sequence of reactions can be outlined as follows: 
At the cathodic surface: 
• 
O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ 
or 
• 
2H2O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ (in less aerated zones) 
Precipitation occurs as: 
• 
Ca²⁺ + CO3²⁻ → CaCO3 ↓ 
• 
Mg²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Mg(OH)2 ↓  [4] 
The extent and rate of deposit formation depend on several factors: 
• 
Water composition, especially the concentrations of Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and CO3²⁻ 
• 
pH at the surface, which is influenced by current density and flow conditions 
• 
Temperature (solubility decreases with increasing temperature) 
• 
Velocity of the surrounding seawater 
In general, calcium carbonate is the more prominent deposit in areas with moderate flow, while 
magnesium hydroxide tends to dominate in low-flow or stagnant areas due to its higher solubility 
threshold. 
These deposits form a secondary barrier to corrosion by: 
• 
Physically blocking the transport of oxygen and aggressive ions (e.g., Cl⁻) 
• 
Further raising the surface pH 
• 
Enhancing the current efficiency of cathodic protection systems 
 


[Page 20]
19 
 
However, the presence of deposits also introduces certain design considerations: 
• 
They can crack or detach in high-velocity zones, exposing bare metal 
• 
They may reduce heat transfer in thermally active components 
• 
In poorly buffered waters, deposition may be incomplete or irregular 
Calcareous deposits have been widely studied in connection with offshore structures, pipelines, and 
ship hulls. They are especially beneficial for passive metals like steel, which otherwise corrode rapidly 
in the absence of protection. The buildup of such layers can significantly extend the service life of 
submerged structures and components. 
Relevance to Shaft Design 
For marine propulsion shaft particularly if cathodic protection (CP) is considered as a corrosion 
prevention strategy understanding the role of calcareous deposits is essential. 
In our design context: 
• 
The shaft operates in 25 °C seawater, a temperature that favors carbonate precipitation 
• 
The seawater contains sufficient calcium and magnesium ions (as shown in Section 2.1), 
supporting deposit formation 
• 
The rotational speed (225 RPM) creates flow velocities that may limit deposit stability on 
certain surfaces 
Key areas where CP may be applied (e.g., at shaft supports, through-hull penetrations, or stern tube 
interfaces) must be designed to accommodate deposit formation. Proper selection of current density, 
anode placement, and surface finishing will influence the formation and adherence of protective layers. 
Furthermore, Duplex 2205, if used, may benefit from the dual action of passivation and calcareous 
deposit formation, further reducing corrosion risk. However, in turbulent zones or splash areas, 
deposits may be unstable indicating a need to combine CP with coatings or design features that 
minimize exposure. 
In summary, effective use of cathodic protection in our shaft design must account for: 
• 
Deposit formation dynamics 
• 
Water chemistry and flow conditions 
• 
Material compatibility and surface design 
These considerations help ensure the long-term durability and reliability of the propulsion system 
under marine operating conditions. 
 
03.1.5 Effect of Seawater Temperature 
Temperature is one of the most influential environmental factors in the corrosion behavior of metals 
immersed in seawater. It directly affects both the physicochemical properties of seawater and the 
electrochemical kinetics of corrosion reactions. Generally, an increase in temperature accelerates 
corrosion rates, particularly for active metals and alloys. 
Several key temperature-dependent effects are observed: 
1. Increased Corrosion Reaction Rates  
Elevated temperatures enhance the rate of electrochemical reactions by increasing ion mobility and 
decreasing solution viscosity. According to the Arrhenius relationship, a 10 °C rise in temperature can 
double the corrosion rate of many metals.
