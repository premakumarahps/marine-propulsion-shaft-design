01. Executive Summary 
This project presents the complete design, analysis, and protection strategy of a corrosion-resistant 
marine propulsion shaft intended for long-term operation in a high-salinity seawater environment. 
The shaft operates within the immersion and splash zones, where chloride exposure, cyclic torsional 
loading, oxygen differentials, and biofouling significantly accelerate localized corrosion and fatigue 
deterioration. 
A systematic design methodology was followed, beginning with a detailed assessment of the marine 
environment, the identification of governing corrosion mechanisms, and quantification of mechanical 
design constraints. Material selection was carried out using a structured Translate–Screen–Rank–
Document approach within ANSYS Granta EduPack, filtering over 1900 candidate materials and 
ultimately selecting Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 due to its high yield strength (~460–510 MPa), 
excellent pitting resistance (PREN > 30), favourable fatigue performance, and superior reliability 
under chloride exposure. 
Mechanical design involved calculating the required outer and inner shaft diameters based on 
combined torsional, bending, and axial loads, supported by class-rule checks using IACS UR M68 and 
DNV-CG-0038. Fatigue and vibration analyses confirmed that the selected hollow shaft geometry (OD 
= 125 mm, ID = 60 mm) provides adequate safety margins against torsional fatigue, lateral whirling, 
and dynamic misalignment. 
To safeguard performance in the aggressive marine environment, a hybrid corrosion-protection 
strategy was developed. This includes duplex stainless steel as the base material, a multilayer coating 
system (epoxy + polyurethane + antifouling), and supplementary cathodic protection aligned with 
DNV and ABS guidelines. Special attention is given to critical regions such as splines, tapers, and 
bearing journals, which require electropolishing or dry-film lubrication rather than paint. 
Manufacturing and QA plans were prepared to ensure dimensional accuracy, structural integrity, and 
operational safety. These include CNC machining, controlled heat treatment, surface preparation, and 
rigorous non-destructive examination (UT, MPI/DPI) followed by runout verification. All processes 
adhere to international standards (e.g., DNV, ABS, ISO 31000). 
Overall, the report integrates engineering design, corrosion science, materials engineering, and marine-
class requirements to deliver a propulsion shaft that is structurally sound, corrosion-resistant, 
manufacturable, and compliant with marine safety regulations.

03. Application Description 
The marine propulsion shaft operates in one of the most aggressive natural environments encountered 
in engineering applications. Its performance and long-term durability are directly influenced by the 
chemical, physical, and biological characteristics of seawater, as well as the electrochemical processes 
that arise at the metal–seawater interface. Understanding these environmental factors is essential for 
selecting appropriate materials, defining geometric features, predicting degradation modes, and 
designing an effective corrosion protection strategy. 
This section provides a detailed description of the operating environment of the shaft and the 
associated corrosion challenges. Section 3.1 presents an environmental analysis of the marine 
conditions relevant to the propulsion system, including salinity, temperature, dissolved gases, flow 
characteristics, biofouling activity, and other parameters that influence corrosion behavior. Section 3.2 
builds on this foundation by examining the corrosion mechanisms most likely to affect marine 
propulsion shafts, such as pitting, galvanic corrosion, crevice corrosion, microbiologically influenced 
corrosion, and corrosion fatigue. 
Together, these analyses establish the environmental context required for subsequent sections on 
material selection, mechanical design, and corrosion protection strategy. They also provide the 
scientific basis for evaluating the suitability of candidate materials such as duplex stainless steels and 
for justifying the adoption of coatings, cathodic protection, or hybrid protection systems in the final 
shaft design. 
 
03. 1 Environmental Analysis of the Marine Operating Environment  
Seawater covers approximately 70% of the Earth’s surface and represents one of the most chemically 
and biologically complex natural environments. It contains nearly every element in the periodic table, 
along with a vast diversity of living organisms, earning it the description of a “living” medium. Due to 
its aggressive nature, seawater is widely regarded as one of the most corrosive environments 
encountered in engineering applications. This chapter explores the critical environmental parameters of 
seawater that influence material degradation, especially corrosion. These factors are essential for 
understanding and predicting material performance in marine systems such as ships, offshore 
platforms, pipelines, and submerged components. The subsections below examine key characteristics 
including salinity, dissolved gases, pH, biological activity, temperature, depth, flow velocity, and 
electrical resistivity, all of which collectively define the corrosive nature of seawater. 
 
03.1.1 Seawater Constituents 
Seawater is widely recognized as one of the most chemically aggressive environments for metallic 
materials, primarily due to its complex composition. Although sodium chloride (NaCl) is often cited as 
the main source of corrosivity, the actual corrosion behavior is influenced more significantly by other 
constituents particularly dissolved oxygen (DO) and pollutants such as sulfides. These components 
critically affect the electrochemical conditions at the metal–seawater interface. 
The total salt content of seawater is expressed as salinity, which refers to the mass of total dissolved 
solids (TDS) in one kilogram of seawater, after oxidation of organic matter and replacement of 
bromine and iodine by chlorine. A related and analytically more accessible parameter is chlorinity, 
which includes the chloride ion concentration as well as the equivalent amount of bromide and iodide.

03.1.5 Effect of Seawater Temperature 
Temperature is one of the most influential environmental factors in the corrosion behavior of metals 
immersed in seawater. It directly affects both the physicochemical properties of seawater and the 
electrochemical kinetics of corrosion reactions. Generally, an increase in temperature accelerates 
corrosion rates, particularly for active metals and alloys. 
Several key temperature-dependent effects are observed: 
1. Increased Corrosion Reaction Rates  
Elevated temperatures enhance the rate of electrochemical reactions by increasing ion mobility and 
decreasing solution viscosity. According to the Arrhenius relationship, a 10 °C rise in temperature can 
double the corrosion rate of many metals. 

--- Page 21 ---
20 
 
2. Reduction in Dissolved Oxygen (DO) Solubility  
As temperature rises, the solubility of DO in seawater decreases, potentially limiting the availability of 
oxygen at the metal surface. However, in natural systems, turbulence and replenishment often 
compensate for this reduction, especially in high-flow conditions. 
3. Acceleration of Localized Corrosion  
Localized forms of corrosion such as pitting and crevice corrosion are particularly sensitive to 
temperature. Many stainless steels exhibit a critical pitting temperature (CPT), above which passive 
films break down rapidly. Therefore, even modest increases in temperature can shift materials from 
safe operation to active corrosion zones. 
4. Influence on Bioactivity  
Higher temperatures promote the growth and metabolic activity of marine organisms, increasing the 
risk of biofouling and microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC). Warm, nutrient-rich waters are 
especially susceptible to biofilm formation. 
5. Effect on Deposit Formation and Solubility  
The solubility of salts such as calcium carbonate and magnesium hydroxide decreases with increasing 
temperature, which can enhance calcareous deposit formation on cathodically protected surfaces. 
However, if flow velocity is high, deposits may be washed away before they adhere. 
The seawater temperature varies by location, season, and depth. Surface waters in tropical regions can 
reach 30 °C or higher, while polar regions remain below 5 °C. Seasonal fluctuations of up to 15 °C are 
common in temperate zones. Moreover, industrial discharge, power plant outfalls, and shallow 
enclosed basins can cause localized thermal elevation, creating corrosion “hot spots” for submerged 
structures. [5] 
Relevance to Shaft Design 
In our project, the marine propulsion shaft is designed to operate in seawater at an average temperature 
of 25 °C, placing it within the moderate to high-temperature range for typical marine environments. 
This has several implications: 
• 
The selected material must withstand enhanced corrosion kinetics. For example, Duplex 2205 
offers significantly better critical pitting temperature (CPT) performance than SS316L, making it 
a more robust choice in warmer seawater. 
• 
Elevated temperature can accelerate localized corrosion in crevices, such as those found around 
keyways, couplings, and seal interfaces. These areas must be carefully designed and, if needed, 
protected with coatings or cathodic protection. 
• 
Reduced DO solubility at higher temperatures may shift corrosion control mechanisms. However, 
the rotational speed (1200 RPM) of the shaft promotes continuous oxygen replenishment through 
turbulence, maintaining corrosion potential. 
• 
Temperature-driven bioactivity can promote biofouling and MIC. This reinforces the need for 
anti-fouling strategies, especially near low-flow regions or during long idle periods. 
• 
If cathodic protection is employed, elevated temperature could enhance calcareous deposit 
formation, aiding in corrosion resistance provided that local flow conditions do not strip away 
forming layers.

03.2.9 Erosion-Corrosion and Cavitation 
Erosion-corrosion results from the combined action of fluid motion and electrochemical attack. In 
marine propulsion shafts, it occurs in areas with high flow velocities, such as near seals, bearings, or 
shaft exits. The mechanical force of the fluid removes protective oxide films, exposing fresh metal to 
rapid corrosion. Cavitation, which involves bubble collapse near the surface, causes microjets that pit 
the surface. 
Stainless steels with insufficient hardness or protection are particularly vulnerable. Loss rates can 
exceed 0.5 mm/year in turbulent flow. Typical damage appears as horseshoe-shaped grooves or pitting 
aligned with flow direction [14], [18]. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Mitigation involves using erosion-resistant materials (e.g., duplex or martensitic stainless steels), 
ensuring laminar flow design, applying hard coatings (e.g., tungsten carbide), and controlling shaft 
alignment to prevent turbulence. Shaft velocity should be monitored to stay below cavitation 
thresholds. 
 
03.2.10 Fretting Corrosion 
Fretting corrosion occurs at contact interfaces under cyclic vibration or small-amplitude oscillation. In 
marine shafts, this typically arises at press-fits, splines, or couplings where relative motion disrupts 
passive films. The exposed metal oxidizes, producing debris that further accelerates wear and 
corrosion [13], [14]. 
This mechanism often serves as a precursor to corrosion fatigue and is worsened by moisture, poor fit 
tolerances, or shaft misalignment. It reduces the fatigue limit and can initiate cracks under moderate 
cyclic stress. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Prevention involves precision machining for tight fits, use of anti-fretting coatings (e.g., MoS₂, PTFE), 
and lubrication. Compressively stressed surfaces (e.g., shot-peened areas) resist fretting better. 
Materials with higher hardness and stable passive films, such as duplex stainless steels, provide greater 
resistance.

04. Material Selection 
The selection of a suitable material for the marine propulsion shaft is a critical step in ensuring safe 
torque transmission, high fatigue life, corrosion resistance, and long-term reliability in seawater 
environments. Because the shaft operates under combined torsional and bending loads while being 
continuously exposed to chloride-rich seawater, the material must satisfy simultaneous mechanical, 
corrosion, and manufacturability constraints. To ensure a systematic and defensible choice, the 
material selection process in this project was carried out using the Ashby-based methodology 
Translate, Screen, Rank, and Document supported by the ANSYS Granta EduPack software, which 
enables quantitative filtering and comparison across a large materials database. 
Design constraints such as minimum yield strength, fatigue strength at 107 cycles, seawater corrosion 
resistance (including PREN ≥ 30), weldability, and hot forming compatibility were applied during the 
screening stage to narrow an initial pool of over 1900 metals to a feasible set of candidates. 
Subsequent ranking using performance indices (strength-to-density, stiffness-to-density, cost-per-unit 
strength) provided a rational basis for comparing options under realistic marine shaft loading 
conditions. 
This section summarizes the material selection workflow, presents the shortlisted candidates produced 
by ANSYS Granta (see Annex A for the full Granta selection report), and discusses their performance 
using the exported ranking charts and datasheets. The full ANSYS Granta selection report and the final 
material datasheet for Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 are included in the Appendix for traceability and 
verification (see Annex B for the material datasheet of Duplex 2205).  
 
04.1 Introduction 
04.1.1 Background and Design Requirement 
Marine propeller shafts operate in a harsh environment: cyclic mechanical loads from torque and 
bending, exposure to saline water (leading to corrosion, pitting, crevice corrosion, stress corrosion 
cracking), and sometimes elevated temperatures or variable loads. The shaft must transmit torque 
reliably, resist fatigue failure and corrosion over long service life, yet remain manufacturable and cost-
effective. 
04.1.2 Objective of Material Selection 
The objective is to find a material that balances the competing demands of strength, durability, 
corrosion resistance, and cost. We employ a structured methodology (translate → screen → rank → 
document) using ANSYS Granta (Granta EduPack or similar) to filter and compare candidate 
materials. [22] 
The final goal is to make a justified recommendation (with supporting data). 
 
04.2 Methodology 
04.2.1 General Material Selection Strategy (Translate – Screen – Rank – Document) 
The methodology used in this study follows a structured, multi-stage approach to material selection 
based on the framework popularized by Ashby and employed in engineering design practice. The four 
main steps are: 
1. Translate: convert real-world design requirements (loads, environment, geometry, constraints) 
into quantitative functions, constraints, objectives, and free variables. 

--- Page 38 ---
37 
 
• Function describes what the component must do (e.g. transmit torque, resist corrosion). 
• Constraints are nonnegotiable property limits (e.g. minimum yield strength, maximum 
allowable corrosion rate). 
• Objectives are performance measures to maximize or minimize (e.g. minimize mass or 
cost, maximize fatigue life). 
• Free variables are the design parameters the engineer may vary (e.g. cross-section, 
thickness). 
This translation step ensures that subsequent filtering and ranking are rooted in the physics and usage 
context of the component.  
2. Screen (or reject): apply the constraints to eliminate materials that cannot meet the basic 
requirements. This reduces the candidate set to only those feasible for the application. Common 
constraints include yield strength, fatigue strength, corrosion resistance, manufacturability, cost 
ceilings, etc. Materials failing any of these constraints are removed. The screening step uses 
property charts or database filters.  
 
3. Rank (or Select / Compare): among the screened materials, compute performance indices 
(also known as merit indices) based on the objectives and free variables. These indices 
(combinations of material properties) allow direct ranking of materials (higher is better, or 
lower if minimizing). You may also handle multiple objectives via trade-offs, coupling 
equations, or multi-stage ranking. Materials are sorted, compared, and candidates are selected.  
 
4. Document (or Support): prepare a record of reasoning, data sources, assumptions, and 
comparisons. Provide justification of the selected material relative to alternatives. Include raw 
data, charts, decision paths, sensitivity analyses, and any caveats. 
This “Translate → Screen → Rank → Document” paradigm helps maintain clarity, traceability, and 
rational decision making. In real cases, iteration is often necessary: if the ranking yields no satisfactory 
solution, one must revisit the translation step (relax or re-interpret constraints, adjust objectives) or 
consider alternate designs. [22], [23], [24], [25]

04.3 Design Requirements for Marine Propeller Shaft 
04.3.1 Functional Requirements 
The propeller shaft transmits torque from the engine to the propeller, while also resisting bending 
forces from propeller thrust, hydrodynamic loading, and shaft misalignment. The component must: 
• Carry a design torque corresponding to the vessel’s power without yielding (minimum yield > 
66 MPa for 100 mm dia). 
• Resist cyclic torsional stresses over long service life (≥ 10⁷ cycles). 
• Maintain alignment and stiffness (torsional twist and bending deflection within tolerances). 
• Operate reliably under combined torque, bending, and thrust loading. 
 
04.3.2 Service Environment (Seawater Corrosion, Fatigue) 
• Seawater at ~25 °C with high salinity is highly aggressive. Shaft material must resist pitting, 
crevice corrosion, and stress-corrosion cracking (SCC). 
• Corrosion fatigue is the dominant failure mode: cyclic stresses in chloride environment 
accelerate crack initiation and growth. 
• Potential galvanic coupling with propeller (bronze, Ni-Al-bronze) or couplings must be 
considered. 
• Surface durability (resistance to wear, corrosion at bearings and seals) is essential.

04.3.3.1 Mechanical constraints 
• Yield strength ≥ 50 ksi (345 MPa) → for 100 mm shaft: ≥ 66 MPa minimum requirement

04.3.3.3 Manufacturing constraints

• Material available in large bar/forging stock. 
04.3.4 Objective (Safe, Cost-Effective, Durable Shaft) 
The selected material must: 
• Maximize service life by combining high fatigue strength and corrosion resistance in seawater. 
• Minimize cost per unit strength (balance between corrosion-resistant Ni alloys and affordable 
steels). 
• Ensure manufacturability with forging, machining, and welding compatibility. 
• Survive pitting and SCC risks through adequate PREN and duplex/austenitic alloy design.

04.6.3 Justification – Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 Is the Final Choice 
The selection of Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 is supported by a balanced combination of mechanical, 
corrosion, and economic performance metrics revealed through the ANSYS Granta screening and 
ranking stages. While austenitic 316LVM demonstrated superior tensile and fatigue strength, its pitting 
resistance (PREN 24 – 31) lies only marginally above the seawater durability threshold, making it 
vulnerable in long-term immersion. Ni–Cr–Mo alloys, although exceptionally corrosion-resistant, were 
eliminated due to their limited mechanical strength and significantly higher cost. 
Duplex 2205 offers an optimal compromise: a yield strength of 66 –74 ksi and fatigue strength of 38 –
42 ksi well above the design minimum combined with a PREN range of 30.5 – 37.8, ensuring reliable 
resistance to pitting, crevice corrosion, and stress corrosion cracking in chloride-rich environments. Its 
balanced ferritic–austenitic microstructure provides superior toughness and fracture resistance 
compared to other candidates, while its weldability and manufacturability remain acceptable for large-
diameter shaft fabrication. Importantly, Duplex 2205 achieves this performance without the substantial 
cost penalty associated with nickel-rich alloys. 
Based on these combined factors, Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 emerges as the most technically and 
economically viable material for the marine propulsion shaft, ensuring long service life and 
operational safety under aggressive marine conditions.

05. Corrosion Protection Strategy 
Marine propulsion shafts operate in one of the most corrosive engineering environments, where 
continuous exposure to high-salinity seawater, oxygen gradients, wet–dry cycling, and hydrodynamic 
stresses can rapidly degrade metallic surfaces. Even alloys with strong intrinsic corrosion resistance 
such as Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 require supplementary protection to prevent localized attack such 
as pitting, crevice corrosion, galvanic coupling, and corrosion-fatigue. A long-term protection strategy 
must therefore integrate electrochemical control and engineered surface treatments to reduce the 
corrosion rate, limit hydrogen uptake, maintain coating integrity, and ensure that the shaft remains 
structurally reliable throughout its service life. 
This section evaluates two complementary approaches: 
• 05.1 – Surface Treatments and Coating Systems, detailing mechanical surface conditioning, 
chemical passivation, multilayer marine coatings, and specialized localized treatments for journals and 
splines. 
• 05.3 – Cathodic Protection (CP), assessing galvanic and ICCP systems, current-demand 
calculations, shaft earthing requirements, and hydrogen-related constraints for duplex steels. 
Together, these methods form a multi-layer corrosion-prevention strategy suitable for long-term 
operation under seawater immersion and splash-zone conditions.

05.1.5 Summary 
Stage 1: Universal Surface Preparation 
Universal Surface Preparation, where the entire shaft undergoes a series of mechanical and chemical 
treatments. This sequence starts with mechanical polishing to refine the surface and is followed by shot 
peening to improve the surface's resistance to fatigue. The shaft is then thoroughly cleaned via alkaline 
degrease and a subsequent rinse to remove all contaminants. Finally, the surface is chemically treated 
with pickling to remove any scale or oxides, which is then immediately followed by a neutralization 
step to halt the acid's chemical reaction, leaving the surface ready for the specialized finishing stages. 
 
Stage 2: Primary Protection and Painting (General Shaft Body) 
For the general shaft body, excluding the bearing journals, the first protective step is passivation using 
citric acid to enhance the material's inherent corrosion resistance. This is followed by the application 
of a high-build marine epoxy coating, which serves as a robust barrier against the seawater 
environment. The third step involves applying a polyurethane top coat to provide durability, UV 
resistance, and a stable finish. Finally, an anti-fouling layer is applied as the outermost protection to 
prevent the attachment and growth of marine organisms. 
 
Stage 3: Specialized Area Treatments 
The bearing journals diverge from the main process immediately after the universal surface 
preparation is complete, receiving only electropolishing as suitable to achieve the required ultra-
smooth finish for optimal bearing performance. Separately, the splined area is the last component to be 
treated, where a dry film lubrication coating must be applied after all other processes are finalized, 
ensuring reduced friction and resistance to wear and galling in this critical mechanical interface. 
 
05.1.6 Coating Breakdown Factor   
 
Typically, the coating breakdown factor represented as fc, the coating breakdown factor is a 
dimensionless metric that measures how likely it is that a protective coating will fail during its design 
life. It is frequently used in the chemical, offshore, and marine industries to assess coating performance 
and the necessity of extra protection, like cathodic protection. 
Low fc provides an effective and durable coating, while coatings with a high fc value may fail and 
require supplementary protection. [44]

05.3 Cathodic Protection (CP) Mechanisms for Marine Propeller Shaft  
05.3.1 Overview 
Cathodic protection reduces corrosion by lowering a structure’s electrochemical potential until anodic 
dissolution becomes negligible. In seawater, CP is applied by either galvanic (sacrificial) anodes or 
impressed current systems (ICCP). ISO 12473 defines the principles, criteria and instrumentation for 
seawater CP, and notes that residual corrosion rates < 10 µm/yr are typically achieved when CP is fully 
effective; it also recognizes hybrid arrangements combining galvanic anodes with ICCP where 
appropriate [49].  
The design framework most widely used in practice is DNV-RP-B401 (current densities, coating 
breakdown factors, anode capacity/utilization, protection criteria), complemented by class guidance for 
ships from ABS (arrangements, shaft bonding/grounding, ICCP placement/controls) [47], [50].  
 
05.3.2 Galvanic (sacrificial) anode CP 
05.3.2.1 Working principle 
Sacrificial anodes are alloys (Al-Zn-In, Zn, rarely Mg in seawater) with more negative free-corrosion 
potentials than the protected steel. They self-deliver current to polarize the structure towards the 
protection potential (≈ -0.80 V vs Ag/AgCl/seawater for steel zones). The current output follows 
Ohm’s law with the driving voltage given by the anode–structure potential difference and limited by 
electrolyte resistance/anode geometry [49].  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
05.3.2.2 Anode alloys and electrochemical properties 
ABS summarizes compositions and practical properties. For aluminum anodes (preferred in seawater 
for high capacity/low mass), typical closed-circuit potentials are ~-1.05 to -1.15 V (Ag/AgCl), with 
practical current capacities ≈ 2500 A·h/kg at ~25 °C (consumption ≈ 3.5 kg/A·yr). “Low-voltage” Al 
alloys (e.g., MIL-DTL-24779B) operate around -0.80 to -0.90 V with lower capacity. Zinc anodes 
operate near -1.00 to -1.05 V with capacity ≈ 760-780 A·h/kg; Mg is rarely used in seawater due to 
overprotection and low efficiency. Temperature derates Al capacity approximately linearly (e.g., 2500 
→ 500 A·h/kg from 25 → 80 °C) [50].

05.3.2.3 Arrangement on ships and appendages 
ABS gives placement and spacing rules for hull zones and, crucially, notes that where a central CP

05.3.2.4 Advantages/limitations  
Advantages: intrinsically fail-safe (no power), simple, predictable consumption (capacity-based 
sizing), minimal topside hardware.  
Limitations: fixed driving voltage (no closed-loop control); potential can drift with 
salinity/temperature/flow; higher mass and drag; risk of over-polarization in local spots (hydrogen 
side-reaction) if anode selection/placement is aggressive; periodic diver replacement. For duplex 
steels, avoiding unnecessarily negative potentials is desirable to minimize hydrogen uptake [48].  
 
05.3.3 Impressed-Current Cathodic Protection (ICCP) 
05.3.3.1 Working principle and hardware 
ICCP uses inert anodes (e.g., platinized-Ti/MMO) fed by a DC power supply; reference electrodes 
(Ag/AgCl) mounted on the hull provide feedback so the controller maintains a set potential. ISO 
12473 explicitly cites the large, adjustable driving voltage of ICCP as a core advantage; it also shows 
secondary anodic reactions (H₂, Cl₂ evolution) that motivate the use of dielectric shields near anodes to 
protect paint/steel. ABS illustrates standard recessed/guarded ICCP anodes and gives minimum 
distances to coatings and shield widths to mitigate chemical attack from gas evolution [49], [50].  
 
05.3.3.2 Control, measurement, and placement 
ABS requires proper placement of reference electrodes between anodes and critical features (e.g., 
propellers), with separation and symmetry rules; it also mandates controller accuracy and logging, and 
describes installation geometry for stern areas [50].  
 
05.3.3.3 Advantages/limitations 
Advantages: Set-point control of hull potential (fine-tuning to avoid over-polarization); low 
hydrodynamic penalty (few, recessed anodes); scalable current output for fouling/waterline variability. 
 
Limitations: Needs power and electronics; requires dielectric shields and careful electrode placement; 
risk of stray-current effects or coating damage if misconfigured. For duplex steels, ICCP’s tight set-
point control directly supports HISC-avoidance by holding potentials near protection levels but not 
excessively negative [50].

06. Mechanical Design 
The mechanical design of a marine propulsion shaft requires a rigorous evaluation of both analytical 
and numerical performance criteria to ensure safe and reliable torque transmission under the combined 
effects of torsion, bending, axial loading, and dynamic excitation. The shaft operates as a critical 
structural component within the propulsion line, transmitting power from the engine to the propeller 
while simultaneously resisting complex loading conditions induced by propeller thrust, hydrodynamic 
forces, and rotational dynamics. This section presents the complete mechanical design methodology 
applied in this project, beginning with the analytical determination of shaft geometry and torque 
transmission capacity, followed by validation through finite-element analysis (FEA). 
Subsection 06.1 describes the derivation of the final shaft geometry through classical strength of 
materials approaches, IACS-based diameter selection formulas, vibration analyses, and fatigue 
calculations. These steps ensure that the chosen shaft dimensions meet both classification-society 
requirements and practical performance targets. Subsection 06.2 presents the FEA-based verification 
of the shaft’s behavior under static and cyclic loads, including stress distribution, deformation patterns, 
natural frequencies, and corrosion-fatigue considerations. Together, these analyses form the foundation 
of the final mechanical design and confirm that the shaft configuration satisfies strength, stiffness, 
vibration, and fatigue life criteria for long-term marine operation. 
 
06.1 Deign of Shaft Geometry and Torque Transmission Calculation  
06.1.1 Introduction 
The design of a marine propulsion shaft is a critical engineering task that involves identifying the 
appropriate geometry and ensuring it can withstand the mechanical stresses experienced during 
operation. This shaft transmits the engine's mechanical power to the propeller, providing the necessary 
thrust for the vessel. As the core component of the shafting system, the shaft design and geometry must 
be robust, able to handle torsional and bending stresses, and resistant to wear and corrosion due to 
prolonged exposure to seawater. 
This section focuses on the shaft design process, where key parameters such as length, diameter, and 
material selection are evaluated. Additionally, the torque transmission capacity and the shaft's ability to 
withstand torsional shear, bending, and fatigue loads are considered, along with the most suitable 
geometry for the shaft. The material properties also play a crucial role in ensuring the shaft’s strength, 
corrosion resistance, and longevity. 
 
06.1.2 Objectives 
➢ Define the design parameters for the marine propulsion shaft based on operational requirements 
and environmental conditions. 
➢ Identify the optimal geometry for the shaft, ensuring it meets the required strength and 
performance standards while minimizing material waste. 
➢ Establish the torque transmission capacity and ensure that the shaft can safely handle the forces 
generated during normal operation. 
➢ Optimize the shaft dimensions to reduce the risk of failure due to torsional or bending stresses 
while maintaining the structural integrity and performance under all operating conditions.

06.1.4.1.7 Bearing  
Bearings are strategically positioned along the shaft line to ensure proper alignment and structural 
support. Their functions include providing static support to the shaft, as well as acting as dynamic 
support to limit lateral vibrations. The number and placement of bearings are determined based on 
shaft alignment calculations and classification society requirements. 
In typical arrangements, two or more bearings are installed within the stern tube to counteract the 
bending moments induced by the propeller. Additional bearings may be required along the 
intermediate shaft, depending on shaft length and operational conditions. While many shafting system 
manufacturers design and assemble complete propulsion systems, bearings themselves are often 
procured from specialized third-party suppliers. 
A critical component of the bearing system is the thrust bearing, which is designed to absorb the axial 
forces generated by the propeller during operation. This bearing prevents axial displacement of the 
shaft while also damping axial vibrations. Thrust bearings may be installed as separate units along the 
shaft line or integrated within the gearbox, depending on the propulsion system configuration.[63] 
 
 
06.1.4.2 Static loads  
The propulsion shaft of a marine vessel is subjected to several types of static loads during operation. 
These loads arise from torque transmission, propeller thrust, and self-weight effects, and they combine 
to produce complex stress states in the shaft material. The main static loads can be summarized as 
follows. 
• 
Torsional shear stress due to the torque transmitted along the shaft. 
• 
Axial normal stress resulting from propeller thrust transmitted to the hull. 
• 
Bending stresses (normal and shear) are induced by transverse loads such as the weight of the 
shaft, propeller, and hydrodynamic effects.

Reason for Using Tensile Strength (𝜎𝐵) in the IACS Minimum Shaft Diameter Formula 
Tensile strength is used in the IACS M68.4 minimum shaft diameter equation because it provides the 
most reliable measure of a shaft material’s ability to withstand the combined effects of torsional shear, 
bending fluctuations, and fatigue. In marine propulsion shafts, failure rarely occurs from static shear 
alone; instead, it is driven by cyclic torsion, variable bending, stress concentrations, and corrosion-
assisted crack initiation. The shear strength and fatigue endurance of steels are well known to correlate 
closely with their minimum specified tensile strength, which is the most consistently certified property 
supplied by manufacturers. By incorporating σᴮ, the IACS formula ensures that the calculated diameter 
accounts not only for shear capacity but also for long-term fatigue performance and real-world 
degradation effects. Thus, tensile strength functions as a practical and conservative parameter to 
prevent shaft failure under marine operating conditions.

06.2 FEA simulation of loading and corrosion fatigue effects 
 
06.2.1 Simulation Methodology and Strategic Approach 
The finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted to evaluate the structural integrity of the propulsion 
shaft under realistic marine operating conditions. A primary objective of this simulation was to predict 
the combined effects of uniform corrosion and localized pitting on the fatigue life of the Duplex 
Stainless Steel 2205 shaft. However, the simultaneous simulation of macroscopic shaft geometry 
(approx. 1.6 m in length) and microscopic pitting defects (< 1.0 mm in depth) presents a significant 
computational challenge known as “multi-scale mesh dependency.” 
Directly meshing a microscopic pit within the full-scale shaft assembly requires an excessive element 
density (exceeding 107 degrees of freedom) to capture the stress singularity at the pit root accurately. 
This approach was determined to be computationally inefficient and prone to mesh distortion errors 
given the available hardware resources. 
To resolve this scale mismatch while ensuring high-fidelity results, a Decoupled Analysis Strategy 
was adopted. This methodology separates the analysis into two distinct phases: 
1. Macro-Level Structural Analysis (Global FEA): A full-scale 3D finite element model of the 
shaft assembly was developed in Abaqus/CAE 2024 to simulate the global stiffness, complex 
contact mechanics at the spline interface, and the stress distribution under nominal operating loads. 
The primary output of this phase was the identification of the exact “Hotspot Node”—the location 
of maximum Von Mises stress in the healthy (Year 0) geometry—and the quantification of stress 
increases due to uniform corrosion thinning (Year 10 Macro state). 

--- Page 122 ---
121 
 
2. Micro-Level Defect Assessment (Analytical Hybrid): Instead of explicitly modeling the pit 
geometry in the FEA mesh, which induces numerical singularities, the localized effect of pitting 
was evaluated analytically. Theoretical Stress Concentration Factors (𝐾𝑡), derived from fracture 
mechanics principles for hemispherical voids in tension/bending fields, were applied to the peak 
stress values obtained from the global FEA simulation. 
This hybrid approach allows for the precise capture of the global load path (Torque, Thrust, and 
Gravity) via simulation, while mathematically quantifying the catastrophic impact of micro-defects 
without compromising solution convergence or computational feasibility. 
 
06.2.2 Geometric Modelling and Assembly Preparation 
To facilitate the finite element analysis, the manufacturing CAD geometry (detailed in Section 07) 
required specific modifications to optimize contact convergence and boundary condition application. A 
parametric “Top-Down” assembly approach was utilized within Siemens Solid Edge to generate the 
analysis-specific geometry. 
 
06.2.2.1 The “Dummy Hub” Technique for Contact Optimization  
Simulating the torque transmission at the spline interface requires a realistic contact model. However, 
introducing a physical clearance gap (typical in manufacturing) induces rigid body motion at the start 
of a static simulation, leading to convergence singularities. To resolve this, a “Dummy Hub” was 
modeled to represent the propeller boss stiffness. 
A Boolean Subtraction method (Inter-part Copy) was employed to generate the hub geometry. The 
shaft spline profile was used as the “Tool Body” to subtract material from a solid ring “Target Body.” 
This operation ensured a perfect Line-to-Line (Zero Clearance) fit between the master and slave 
surfaces. This geometric conformity is critical for the Abaqus contact algorithm to establish immediate 
nodal contact without artificial stabilization.

06.2.2.2 Virtual Bearing Definition via Surface Splitting  
Modeling physical bearing components (rollers, races, and cages) introduces unnecessary 
computational cost for a shaft fatigue analysis. Instead, a Split Surface technique was used to define 
“Virtual Bearing” locations directly on the shaft geometry.

Bearing 1 and Bearing 2. These split surfaces provide discrete geometric regions for the application of 
displacement boundary conditions (𝑈1, 𝑈2 = 0) in the FEA pre-processor, accurately representing the

06.2.3 Finite Element Model Setup (Pre-processing) 
The pre-processing phase involved defining the material behavior, contact physics, and boundary 
conditions required to simulate the operational environment of the propulsion shaft. All setup tasks 
were performed within the Abaqus/CAE 2024 environment. 
 
06.2.3.1 Material Definition and Inertial Properties  
The shaft and dummy hub were assigned the material properties of Duplex Stainless Steel 2205. 
Given that the analysis focuses on High Cycle Fatigue (HCF) where the component operates well 
within the elastic limit, a Linear Elastic material model was utilized. 
• Young’s Modulus (𝐸): 200 GPa 
• Poisson’s Ratio (𝜈): 0.3 
• Density (𝜌): 7.8 × 10−9 ton/mm3 (7800 kg/m3)  
The definition of density was critical to enable the accurate calculation of self-weight deflection 
(sagging) under gravity loads. 
 
06.2.3.2 Interaction Physics: The Tie Constraint  
To simulate the rigid interference fit between the propeller hub and the shaft splines, a Tie Constraint 
was formulated. A standard “Surface-to-Surface” contact was deemed unsuitable due to the potential 
for rigid body motion singularities during the initial analysis steps. 
Instead, the Hub Inner Splines (Slave) were kinematically tied to the Shaft Splines (Master). A critical 
modification was applied to the Position Tolerance, which was manually set to 0.01 mm rather than 
using the computed default. This setting ensured a robust mathematical bond between the mating 
surfaces, preventing node distortion while guaranteeing zero relative motion at the interface. 
 
06.2.3.3 Kinematic Couplings and Reference Points  
To distribute loads realistically without inducing artificial stress concentrations at load application 
points, the Reference Point (RP) Method was employed. Kinematic Couplings were created to link 
abstract control points to the geometric surfaces:

5. Bearing RPs: Located at the shaft axis, coupled to the specific “Split Surfaces” defined in the

Boundary conditions were applied directly to the Reference Points to simulate the constraints imposed

• Hub Constraint: Rotation about the X-axis (𝑈𝑅1) was fixed to provide a reaction against the

• Bearing 2 (Support Bearing): Constrained in Radial (𝑈2, 𝑈3) directions only. Axial

06.2.4 Mesh Sensitivity Analysis and Computational Optimization 
Given the large aspect ratio of the propulsion shaft (1.6 m length vs. 5 mm fillet radii), a uniform mesh 
density was determined to be computationally inefficient. A uniform fine mesh sufficient to capture the 
stress concentration at the spline fillets would result in an excessive element count, exceeding 
available computational resources (RAM/CPU). Therefore, a mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted 
to establish an optimized discretization strategy that balances geometric accuracy with solution 
feasibility. 
 
06.2.4.1 Element Type Selection  
The geometry features complex curvature at the spline teeth and transition fillets which are prone to 
shear locking if modeled with first-order elements. Consequently, C3D10 (10-node Quadratic 
Tetrahedral) elements were selected for the entire assembly. These second-order elements provide 
superior convergence for curved geometries and stress gradients compared to linear tetrahedra (C3D4), 
ensuring accurate capture of the bending and torsional stress fields. 
 
06.2.4.2 Mesh Sensitivity Study  
An iterative convergence study was performed to determine the required element density at critical 
stress concentration regions: 
• Iteration 1 (Uniform Coarse): A global seed size of 25 mm was applied. This resulted in 
significant element distortion at the spline-to-shaft transition and failed to capture the curvature 
of the 5 mm fillets, rendering stress results unreliable.

06.2.4.3 The “7-Zone” Partitioning Strategy  
To implement the findings of the sensitivity analysis, the shaft geometry was partitioned into seven 
distinct zones using datum planes (as defined in the manufacturing design). A variable seeding plan 
was applied to creating a smooth transition from coarse to fine mesh: 
• Zone 2 (Critical Hotspot - Spline/Taper): A global size of 3.0 mm was applied to the spline 
region. Crucially, 1.0 mm Local Seeds were applied to the external spline fillet and internal 
transition fillet edges. This ensures at least 5 elements across the radius of the curvature, 
satisfying standard FEA convergence criteria for stress concentrations. 
• Zone 1 (Thread): Meshed at 5.0 mm to capture the load application surface accurately. 
• Zones 4 & 6 (Bearing Seats): Meshed at 5.0 mm to provide sufficient nodal density for the 
application of rigid boundary conditions. 
• Zone 3 (Buffer Zone): A transition mesh of 8.0 mm was utilized between the Hotspot (3 mm) 
and the bearing seat to prevent abrupt changes in element size that could induce numerical 
artifacts. 
• Zones 5 & 7 (Non-Critical Gaps): The long, uniform sections of the shaft were meshed at 
20.0 mm. Since these regions experience uniform nominal stress, this coarse mesh significantly 
reduced the total degree of freedom count without compromising global stiffness accuracy. 
Outcome: This optimized strategy resulted in a high-fidelity model comprising approximately 1.2 
million elements. The selective refinement allowed for the precise capture of the peak Von Mises

06.2.7 Analytical Fatigue Assessment and Failure Prediction 
The Finite Element Analysis (FEA) results in Section 06.2.6 provided the “Macro-Stress” state of the 
corroded shaft, accounting for the global reduction in cross-sectional area due to uniform corrosion. 
However, FEA alone cannot efficiently predict failure caused by microscopic pitting without 
computationally prohibitive mesh densities. 
Therefore, this final phase employs a Fracture Mechanics and S-N Approach to analytically 
superimpose the effect of pitting corrosion onto the FEA results. This hybrid method calculates the 
Effective Stress (𝜎𝑒𝑓𝑓) at the pit root and compares it against the degraded fatigue strength of Duplex 
2205 in seawater to predict the remaining service life.

08.5.1 Setup and Mounting 
The shaft is securely mounted on the CNC lathe, with the inner bore referenced using the expanding 
mandrel to maintain axial and radial concentricity between the bore and external features. The taper 
turning attachment or CNC interpolation cycles are programmed using the shaft’s detailed CAD model 
and drawings for accuracy. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
08.5.2 Machining the Taper 
• 
The taper is machined to a 1:16 ratio (or roughly 3.58° angle) taper as per marine shaft 
standards, reducing the shaft diameter smoothly from approximately 125 mm at the shoulder to 
106 mm at the tip over the specified 300 mm length. 
• 
During taper cutting, a light finish allowance of about 0.5 mm is maintained for later finishing 
operations.  
• 
This taper size conforms to marine industry standards and class society requirements for 
propeller fittings. 
• 
Rough passes remove bulk material with depths of cut around 1–2 mm and feeds slowed to 
0.3–0.5 mm/rev to minimize vibration and maintain dimensional control. 
• 
Final passes employ slower feeds and cutting speeds (0.1–0.2 mm/rev feeds, 60–80 m/min 
speeds), targeting tight tolerances within ±0.02 mm and a fine surface roughness of Ra ≤ 0.8 
μm.[94] 
08.5.3 Machining Shoulders and Blend Radii 
• 
The shoulder defines the transition zone between the hollow cylindrical section and the solid 
taper, designed for smooth stress flow and to serve as an assembly reference. 
• 
A fillet radius or blend radius of about 5 mm is machined at this junction to reduce stress 
concentrations during torsional loading. 
• 
These radii are critical to preventing fatigue cracks and ensuring the shaft withstands 
operational stresses over time. 
• 
Machining tools with polished carbide inserts and appropriate nose radii are used to achieve a 
smooth contour linking the cylindrical and tapered sections. 
08.5.4 Quality Assurance 
• 
Dimensional validation is conducted using high-precision contact or optical measurement 
instruments to verify taper angles, diameters, and shoulder geometries.

09.6 Safety Plan 
The safety plan addresses both the safe manufacturing/installation of the shaft and the operational 
safety throughout the shaft’s service life. It covers handling of the heavy shaft, identification of 
potential failure modes (corrosion, fatigue) and their mitigation, in-service monitoring to preempt 
issues, and precautions during fabrication and cathodic protection (CP) system installation. The aim is 
to minimize the risk of accidents, component failure, or environmental hazards, thereby protecting 
personnel, equipment, and the vessel. 
 
09.6.1 Handling, Installation, and Operational Safety 
09.6.1.1 Handling and Lifting  
Marine propulsion shafts are large, heavy components (often hundreds of kilograms or more), so safe 
handling is paramount. During manufacturing and assembly:  
• A detailed lifting plan is used whenever the shaft is moved. Certified lifting equipment 
(overhead cranes, slings, shackles) with adequate capacity (SWL well above the shaft weight) 
are employed. The shaft should be slung at balanced lift points (often a shaft will have eye 
bolts or a pallet for lifting) to avoid sudden shifts. Tag lines are used to guide the load and keep 
personnel clear of pinch points.  
• Only trained riggers operate the crane; all personnel in the vicinity must stand clear of the load 
path. Hard hats, steel-toe shoes, and gloves are mandatory. The shaft is never to be lifted over 
or near personnel – the area is barricaded during lifts.  
• When setting the shaft down, ensure chocks or V-blocks are in place to prevent it from rolling. 
The shaft is secured on wooden or rubber-covered V-blocks both in storage and during 
machining to avoid any unexpected movement.  
• During machining or any rotational operation (like turning on lathe), machine guards and 
interlocks are in place. Operators ensure no loose clothing or items that could entangle. A lock-
out/tag-out (LOTO) procedure is followed for the machining equipment during tool changes or 
inspections – i.e., power is cut and the spindle is verified stationary before approaching the 
shaft in the machine. 
09.6.1.2 Installation Safety 
Installing the shaft into the vessel (or test rig) involves careful alignment and bolting:  
• Prior to installation, a Job Hazard Analysis is done to identify risks (such as heavy lifts in the 
engine room, working in confined spaces, or high locations at the stern). Appropriate measures 
like ventilation (for confined stern tube spaces), lighting, and communication methods are set. 
• The vessel’s stern tube or bearing supports must be ready to receive the shaft. Edges are 
checked to be free of burrs that could snag. When inserting the shaft through bearings or seals, 
come-alongs or hydraulic jacks are often used to pull the shaft slowly – this avoids sudden 
movements. Hands are kept clear of any gap between shaft and hull; tools like pushers are used 
instead of fingers to maneuver the shaft.  
• Alignment: During coupling to the engine or gearbox, alignment pins and jacks are used to line 
up the flanges. Bolts are inserted and tightened sequentially. This is done with the engine 
locked out (no chance of rotation) to ensure safety. If any on-site machining (like final facing 
of coupling flange) is needed, portable machines are used with proper guarding.

10. Discussion 
The results of this project highlight the complex relationship between mechanical design, material 
behavior, and environmental degradation in marine propulsion applications. The selection of 
Duplex Stainless Steel 2205 emerged as a pivotal decision, providing significantly improved resistance 
to pitting, crevice corrosion, and stress corrosion cracking compared to alternatives such as SS316L. 
This conclusion is supported by the PREN-based corrosion screening and Granta property analysis 
contained within the report. 
The mechanical design calculations demonstrate that the combined torsion–bending–axial loading 
condition produces stress levels well within the allowable limits for Duplex 2205, even under 
conservative assumptions. Fatigue calculations based on the axial amplitude at 10⁷ cycles show that 
safety factors remain comfortably above unity. Likewise, torsional vibration and lateral whirling 
analyses indicate that the shaft’s natural frequencies remain safely separated from operational speeds, 
reducing the risk of dynamic instability. This aligns with class-rule interpretations from IACS UR M68 
and DNV-CG-0038. 
This project also revealed a key challenge: corrosion protection must be integrated, not treated as 
an isolated step. Cathodic protection (CP) alone cannot fully protect the shaft due to splash-zone wet–
dry cycling, crevice regions near couplings, and MIC (microbial influenced corrosion) effects. 
Conversely, coatings alone may degrade under mechanical wear or turbulent flow. The hybrid strategy 
adopted in Section 05 integrating multi-layer epoxy/PU/antifouling coatings with controlled CP 
potential addresses these limitations and reduces long-term maintenance demands. 
From a manufacturing perspective, the sequencing of machining and heat-treatment steps proved 
crucial. Duplex 2205 undergoes dimensional distortion during solution annealing (as detailed in the 
report), necessitating that spline machining, threading, and critical taper finishing occur after heat 
treatment to preserve geometric accuracy. This aligns with industry standards and avoids premature 
tolerance deviations that compromise fit and fatigue life. 
Class-rule alignment (DNV, ABS, LR) also shaped the design. Requirements for bearing alignment, 
runout limits, cyclic loading margins, and vibration separation ratios were referenced to validate the 
design. The chosen geometry satisfies these criteria, demonstrating compatibility with practical 
marine-industry requirements beyond purely theoretical design. 
Finally, the surface treatment workflow universal preparation, localized polishing, dry-film lubrication 
on splines, and a final multilayer coating—provides a coherent, realistic protection pathway. The NDE 
and QA process ensures traceability and quality assurance, reflecting the real-world expectations of 
classification societies and shipyards. 
Overall, the project successfully integrates mechanical integrity, corrosion science, manufacturability, 
and regulatory compliance. The design represents a balanced, optimized solution, though further 
refinements are possible through deeper dynamic simulation or operational monitoring.
